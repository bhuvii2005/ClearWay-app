# WHO 2021 air quality guideline levels

In September 2021 the World Health Organization updated its global air quality guidelines for the first time since 2005. The new levels are lower than before, because newer research showed that air pollution harms health at concentrations previously thought to be safe. The guideline levels are the concentrations below which the evidence of harm is weakest. They are advice, not law: many countries set higher legal limits.

## Safe levels for fine particles (PM2.5)

PM2.5 means particles 2.5 micrometres across or smaller. The WHO guideline for PM2.5 is an annual average of 5 µg/m³ and a 24-hour average of 15 µg/m³. The 24-hour level should not be exceeded on more than three or four days a year. There is no level of PM2.5 known to be completely harmless, so lower is always better, but staying under 15 µg/m³ for the day is the WHO's safe-level target.

## Coarse particles (PM10)

PM10 covers particles up to 10 micrometres, which includes dust and pollen as well as PM2.5. The guideline is an annual average of 15 µg/m³ and a 24-hour average of 45 µg/m³.

## Nitrogen dioxide (NO₂)

NO₂ comes mostly from road traffic, especially diesel engines. The guideline is an annual average of 10 µg/m³ and a 24-hour average of 25 µg/m³. Levels are usually highest beside busy roads and fall off quickly a few streets away.

## Ozone (O₃)

Ground-level ozone forms when sunlight acts on traffic and industrial fumes, so it peaks on sunny afternoons. The guideline is an 8-hour average of 100 µg/m³, and a peak-season average of 60 µg/m³.

## Interim targets

For places far above the guidelines, the WHO also sets stepping-stone interim targets. For example, the interim targets for 24-hour PM2.5 run from 75 down to 25 µg/m³ before the 15 µg/m³ guideline.
